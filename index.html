Frequently Asked Questions

New to debate motions? This page answers the most common questions about what motions are, how they work, and how BP and KP formats differ.

📌 General

A debate motion is a statement or proposition that debaters argue for or against. It defines the topic of the debate. One side (the Government or Proposition) argues in favor of the motion, while the other side (the Opposition) argues against it.

Example: "This House would ban private tutoring." — the Government argues why banning it is good; the Opposition argues why it should not be banned.

A motion definition clarifies what the motion means so both sides debate the same thing. A good definition includes:

  • Key terms — define unclear or contested words in the motion
  • Scope — explain what the motion covers (which country, which group, which time period)
  • The model — describe what the policy or change would look like in practice

Example: For "THW ban private tutoring", you would define "private tutoring" (paid, one-on-one or small group academic coaching outside school), clarify the scope (e.g., Myanmar, all levels), and describe how the ban works (who enforces it, what counts as a violation).

A definition should be fair and reasonable — not so narrow that it makes the motion impossible to oppose, and not so broad that it changes the spirit of the motion.

These are standard shorthand prefixes used in debate motions:

  • THW — This House Would (proposes a policy or action)
  • THBT — This House Believes That (makes a factual or value claim)
  • THS — This House Supports (expresses backing for a position or movement)
  • THFW — This House Would Further (builds on an existing policy or situation)

"This House" refers to the debating chamber as a whole — it's a traditional parliamentary phrase.

🏛️ British Parliamentary (BP) Format

British Parliamentary (BP) is the most widely used competitive debate format in the world, used at WUDC (World Universities Debating Championship) and many regional tournaments.

A BP round has 4 teams of 2 speakers — two Government teams and two Opposition teams — all competing against each other in a single round. Speakers give 7-minute speeches, with Points of Information (POIs) allowed during the middle 5 minutes.

  • Opening Government (OG) — sets the definition and the main case
  • Opening Opposition (OO) — opposes the motion
  • Closing Government (CG) — extends OG's case with a new argument
  • Closing Opposition (CO) — extends OO's case with a new argument

BP motions generally fall into these main types:

  • Policy motions — propose a specific action or law (e.g., "THW ban X", "THW give Y to Z")
  • Belief/value motions — argue for a claim or principle (e.g., "THBT economic growth matters more than equality")
  • Support motions — express backing for a movement or trend (e.g., "THS the rise of nationalist movements in Southeast Asia")
  • Comparative motions — argue that one option is better than another (e.g., "THBT X is preferable to Y")
  • Actor motions — put debaters in the role of a specific actor making a decision (e.g., "THW, as the UN, impose sanctions on X")

BP motions are typically released only 15 minutes before the round, so preparation speed and flexibility are key skills.

⚖️ Karl Popper (KP) Format

Karl Popper (KP) debate is a research-based format designed to encourage critical thinking on complex social, political, and ethical issues. It is widely used at the high school level and named after the philosopher Karl Popper.

A KP round has 2 teams of 3 speakers — one Proposition team and one Opposition team. Unlike BP, KP allows preparation time before the tournament, so teams research their topic in advance.

Each speaker gives a constructive speech (6 minutes) and a rebuttal or cross-examination, with cross-examination periods between speeches where the opposing team asks questions.

KP motions are usually written as clear, resolvable statements. Common types include:

  • Policy resolutions — propose a specific government or institutional policy (e.g., "Myanmar should prioritize X over Y")
  • Value resolutions — argue for a moral or philosophical principle (e.g., "Justice matters more than stability in post-conflict societies")
  • Fact resolutions — argue whether something is true or false (e.g., "Economic sanctions are an effective tool for political change")

KP motions are pre-announced — teams know the topic days or weeks before the tournament and can prepare evidence, research, and structured arguments in advance.

The key differences between BP and KP motions:

  • Preparation: BP motions are given only 15 minutes before the round. KP motions are announced in advance, allowing full research preparation.
  • Phrasing: BP motions often use "This House Would/Believes" language. KP motions are often phrased as direct resolution statements.
  • Scope: BP motions cover a very wide range of global, abstract, or hypothetical scenarios. KP motions tend to be more grounded and research-focused.
  • Evidence: In KP, debaters are expected to bring evidence and statistics to support their arguments. In BP, logical reasoning and examples carry more weight than cited sources.
  • Format: BP has 4 teams competing simultaneously; KP is a direct 2-team contest.
🇲🇲 Myanmar Context

Most international debate motion banks focus on global or Western contexts. Myanmar debaters often have to adapt foreign motions or debate topics that feel distant from their own experience.

This library was built to fill that gap — offering motions that reflect real issues in Myanmar: federalism, ethnic reconciliation, social media regulation, environmental policy, education access, and more.

Debating locally relevant topics helps debaters develop stronger arguments, connect theory to real-world stakes, and engage more meaningfully with the issues that shape their communities.

For Debaters & Coaches

Motion Guide

A practical guide to understanding, approaching, and mastering debate motions — with a focus on the most challenging motion type of all.

If you have been debating for a while, you already know the feeling. The motion drops. You read it. It starts with "This House Would…" — and suddenly you are not just arguing a belief or a value. You are being asked to do something. To build a policy. To make it work.

Policy motions are a different beast. And they are not placed at the beginning of a tournament by accident. You find them in semifinals. You find them in finals. Because they are the motions that separate good debaters from great ones.

What Makes Policy Motions Different

Most motions ask you to argue. Policy motions ask you to govern. When a motion calls for an action — a ban, a law, a program, a reform — the Government team cannot simply say the idea is good. They have to show that it works.

This is what makes policy motions both exciting and dangerous. A strong belief argument can carry a team through a values debate. But in a policy round, a weak mechanism breaks your entire case.

What Every Policy Must Answer

When you face a policy motion, think of yourself as a policymaker, not just a debater. Your policy needs to answer six questions before you walk into the room:

01
What is the action?

Be precise. Vague policies lose rounds. Define exactly what your government is doing.

02
Who carries it out?

Name the actor — which ministry, institution, or body is responsible. This is your focal point.

03
How does it happen?

Describe the mechanism. How is the policy enforced, funded, or implemented?

04
What is the timeframe?

Policies do not happen overnight. Show that your model is realistic by acknowledging when and how change unfolds.

05
Why does it matter?

Connect the policy to a real harm you are solving or a real good you are creating. The "why" is your moral anchor.

06
What are the impacts?

Think beyond the immediate effect. Who benefits, who is affected, and what changes in the world because of this policy?

Why Debaters Struggle With Policy Motions

The most common mistake is treating a policy motion like a values debate. Teams spend their speeches arguing why something should happen and forget to explain how it actually will. The Opposition tears them apart not on principle, but on practicality.

The second mistake is building a policy that is too ambitious or too vague. A policy that tries to change everything tends to prove nothing. Keep the model focused and defensible.

The third mistake is ignoring unintended consequences. A good Opposition will find them. A great Government team names them first and explains why the policy is still worth it.

Treat It Like a Challenge, Not a Threat

Policy motions are hard. That is the point. They reward teams who prepare seriously, think structurally, and can build something under pressure. When you see a policy motion in a semifinal, do not panic. See it as your chance to show that you can not only argue — you can lead.

Build the policy. Defend it. Own it.

About This Library

What This Library Offers

This library is a free and open collection of debate motions developed for Myanmar's social, political, economic, and cultural contexts. Every motion is available in both English and Burmese and is organized by format (BP or KP) and level (High School or University), making it easier for debaters, trainers, and clubs to find relevant motions.

Why This Library Matters

For Myanmar debaters, the places where we can discover, research, and collect debate motions are still limited. While motions are used across schools, universities, and debate clubs, there has not been a dedicated platform where Myanmar debaters can easily access and share them.

This library was created to help fill that gap. It serves as a shared space where debaters can find motions, contribute new ones, and learn from debates taking place across different communities.

By making motions more accessible, we hope to encourage the exchange of ideas and approaches between debaters from different regions and institutions. A motion debated in one place can inspire new perspectives in another. This library exists to support that exchange and to strengthen Myanmar's debate community through shared knowledge and resources.
